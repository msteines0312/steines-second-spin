Review

Kendrick Lamar walked into his first major label album in five years and decided to make a therapy record. Not metaphorically — the album opens with a therapist's voice, follows Kendrick through a couch-session reckoning with generational trauma and ego, and doesn't resolve neatly at the end. That discomfort is the whole point.

Mr. Morale & The Big Steppers is Kendrick's most personal album, and also his most difficult. Where good kid, m.A.A.d city told a story and To Pimp a Butterfly made an argument, Mr. Morale just sits with things — addiction, infidelity, the way pain gets passed down through families, the impossibility of being a symbol when you're still figuring yourself out. It's messy in a way his previous albums weren't, and that messiness is either the whole appeal or the main complaint, depending on who you ask.

The production shifts register constantly. "Die Hard" feels like early evening luxury rap; "N95" is jagged and uncomfortable; "Savior" is a sermon that critiques the idea of Kendrick as savior. Disc two, The Big Steppers, is darker and stranger — "Mr. Morale" and "Mother I Sober" are among the most raw things he's ever put to tape. The album doesn't build toward a catharsis because catharsis isn't the point. The point is the work itself, the looking directly at it.

It's not his best album. It might be his most important one.
